My avatar has laser eyes and a fake name, and my mother would tell you I'm the most honest man she knows.

Those two facts are not in tension. That's the part most people get backwards, and it's worth an essay, because sooner or later everyone who goes deep on Bitcoin faces the same fork in the road: *do I say these things under my real name?*

I chose the mask. Here's why — and here's what it costs, because it isn't free.

## The wrench

There's an old comic about security that every bitcoiner eventually learns to love. A hacker wants your encrypted data. Option one: crack the encryption with a million-dollar cluster. Option two: hit you with a five-dollar wrench until you give up the password.

The wrench always wins. Which means the cheapest security upgrade in existence is making sure nobody knows there's anything worth swinging at.

When you write about money — not stocks-and-bonds money, but *I hold an asset that can't be frozen, clawed back, or quietly confiscated* money — your name becomes a target vector. Not hypothetically. There is a public, running list of physical attacks on people who were known to hold bitcoin, and the one thing the victims share isn't wealth. It's that *somebody knew.*

Which is why the oldest house rule in this culture goes: **talk about bitcoin, but never talk about *your* bitcoin.** A pseudonym is just that rule taken to its logical conclusion — I can talk about bitcoin all day long, because RadVladdy doesn't have a street address.

So the mask isn't paranoia. It's arithmetic. And it isn't for me, mostly — it's for the people who live in my house and did not sign up for my opinions.

## The most famous mask in finance

Here's the thing that settles the "pseudonyms are shady" argument forever: the most consequential monetary invention in centuries was shipped by someone wearing one.

Satoshi Nakamoto published the design for Bitcoin, ran it for two years, and walked away without ever showing a face. And the pseudonym wasn't incidental to Bitcoin's success — it was *load-bearing*. No founder to subpoena. No CEO to flatter, threaten, or flip. No great man whose biography could be used to judge the work instead of the work. The idea had to stand entirely on its own, and seventeen years of attacks later, it still does.

That's the cypherpunk inheritance in one sentence: *judge the code, not the face.* The people who built the tools this whole space runs on understood that privacy isn't the same thing as secrecy — privacy is the power to selectively reveal yourself to the world. A mask isn't a wall. It's a door you control.

## A pseudonym is not anonymity

This is the distinction that matters, so let me draw it sharply.

**Anonymity** is a burner. No name, no history, no consequences. Anonymous accounts can say anything because they've staked nothing — which is exactly why nobody trusts them.

**A pseudonym** is the opposite of a burner. It's a name that accrues history it can never shed. RadVladdy has receipts going back to January 2019 — the old posts are in [this site's archive](/archive) with their original dates, including the embarrassing ones, *especially* the embarrassing ones. Every essay adds to a reputation that took years to build and would take one bad act to torch. That's not "no skin in the game." That's *all* skin in the game — just a different skin.

And this is where the tools got good. On Nostr, my words aren't attached to a name at all — they're signed by keys. My identity is verifiable against my own domain. My posts can carry cryptographic timestamps. You don't have to wonder whether the mask is consistent; you can *check*. A pseudonym in 2026 isn't a guy in a trench coat. It's a public key with a track record.

## What the mask costs

Honest ledger, because every choice has a bill.

The mask means the work can never feed my real-world resume. Whatever this site builds, the other name gets none of the credit — at dinner parties I am, professionally speaking, very boring. It means discipline, forever: pseudonymity is like custody, a practice rather than a purchase, and one careless leak is permanent. There's no support line that can rotate my name like a key. It means odd little frictions you'd never think of — every photo checked, every payment rail split in two, a mental map of which world each account lives in.

And it means trusting that the work is enough. No face to be charming with. No credentials to lean on. If an essay lands, it landed on its own legs.

I've come to think that last cost is secretly the whole benefit.

## Judge the work

So that's the deal I've made, and the deal I'm offering you, reader.

I won't show you a face. You shouldn't care. Faces are how legacy finance does trust — a firm handshake, a nice suit, a name on the building — and that system produced the bailouts, the money printer, and the strong incentive to never say anything true out loud. I'd rather give you the other thing: years of receipts, posts that stand or fall on their own, keys you can verify, and predictions left up where they can age badly in public.

The mask isn't hiding from accountability. The mask is *maximum* accountability — concentrated on the only thing that should ever have counted: the work.
