There's a piece of paper in your pocket tonight with an eagle on it, a pyramid, a floating disembodied eye, and the signatures of people who work for the government. It's paying for the fireworks. We call it the almighty American dollar.

It might be the least American thing you own.

I don't mean that as a bumper sticker. I mean it close to literally. The men who signed the Declaration 250 years ago wrote a specific, weighable definition of the word *dollar* into the founding law of the country — and the thing in your wallet is not it. Somewhere between then and now, somebody quietly swapped the contents and left the label on the jar.

So here's what I want to work out with you this Fourth of July: what did the Founders actually mean when they wrote "dollar"? How did we end up holding something they'd have found not just unconstitutional but a little insulting? And why does a money invented by an anonymous stranger on the internet have a stronger claim to 1776 than the note with the flag printed on it?

Stick with me. This one ruins a barbecue in the best possible way.

## The word they carved into the law

The Founders had just watched a currency die. They'd printed the Continental to fund the Revolution, printed way too much, and watched it collapse so completely that "not worth a Continental" became the era's insult for anything worthless. They learned the lesson in real time: a government that can print its own money will, and the people holding it eat the loss.

So when they built the frame, they bolted the money down. The Constitution gives Congress the power to "coin Money" (Article I, Section 8) — *coin*, a physical verb, not *print*. It forbids the states from making ["any Thing but gold and silver Coin a Tender in Payment of Debts"](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/article-1/section-10/) (Article I, Section 10). And it uses the word "dollar" — [ten dollars in Article I, Section 9](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/article-1/section-9/), twenty in the Seventh Amendment — without ever redefining it, because everyone knew: a dollar was the Spanish silver coin already jingling in every colonial pocket.

Three years later they wrote the weight down. The Coinage Act of 1792 defined a dollar as **371.25 grains of pure silver.** Not a promise. A quantity of metal you could put on a scale.

That was the point. Bitcoin pioneer Trace Mayer — a lawyer who wrote his law-school thesis on American monetary history — [puts it better than I can](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0qFTf6OAgyo): sound money "belongs in the same class as constitutions and bills of rights." Money you can't print isn't a financial detail. It's a limit on power. The Founders put it right in the frame, on purpose, because they'd just felt what happens without it.

## Somebody swapped the contents

Now go get a dollar and read it. It does not say "silver." It does not say "gold." Across the top it says **Federal Reserve Note.** That's not branding. That's a confession.

A *note* is an IOU — this one issued by the Federal Reserve, created in 1913, whose twelve regional banks are owned by the private member banks in their districts. In 1933 the government cut the everyday link to gold. In 1971, Nixon cut the last thread, and the dollar became a promise printed by the very concentration of financial power the Founders spent a revolution trying to escape.

The label still says "dollar." The silver left the building generations ago. And the tell: since 1913 that "dollar" has lost more than 97% of what it could buy. The coin the Constitution described was designed to be un-dilutable. The note that replaced it is designed to be diluted. They're opposites wearing the same name.

## The tax you never got to vote on

This is the part that actually belongs to the Fourth of July. The whole fight came down to five words: *taxation without representation.* Not "taxes are bad" — the insult was being charged without a vote.

Here's Trace Mayer describing what a printable money does to you: it "enables confiscation through inflation, which is a form of taxation without representation — the expropriation of wealth from the citizenry without their consent." When the money supply doubles and your paycheck doesn't, someone transferred your purchasing power to whoever got the new money first. No line item, no ballot, no representative to yell at. We fought a war to stop being taxed without a vote. Then we adopted a money that does it automatically.

## Rules over rulers

Bitcoin has a hard cap: 21 million, not one satoshi more. No board can vote to print extra. It's nobody's IOU — no promise involved, only math that thousands of independent computers check every ten minutes. It's the un-swappable version of what the Founders wrote down.

Natalie Brunell said it cleanest [on the Fourth this morning](https://x.com/natbrunell/status/2073375440316555600): Bitcoin is American "because it embodies the principles America was founded on" — liberty, private property, checks on centralized power, and the one that stops me every time: **rules over rulers.** That's the whole American bet in three words. Not "trust good kings" — *bind every king with rules he can't break.* The Constitution tried to do that to political power with parchment. Bitcoin does it to monetary power with code, and code doesn't get talked out of it.

## The most American thing on the table

So tonight, when the sky lights up and somebody waves the little flag on the little napkin, look at the note in your hand. It has all the symbols. It's just missing the idea.

The most American thing you can own this Fourth of July isn't the paper with the flag on it. It's the money those men would actually recognize: something with a fixed, honest definition that no ruler can edit while you sleep. They carved that principle into the law, watched it get swapped out from under their descendants, and never got to see it come back.

We do. That's the fireworks worth watching. ⚡
