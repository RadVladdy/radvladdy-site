Here's a confession from a man with a sovereignty blog: if I died tonight, my wife wouldn't inherit my bitcoin.

She'd inherit a puzzle. A very secure puzzle, lovingly constructed over many years by a paranoid man she trusted, who is no longer available for questions.

I've spent a long time getting good at keeping people *out*. It took me embarrassingly long to notice that I'd built something my own family might not be able to get *into* — and that every bitcoiner I respect has quietly built the same thing. So let me give the problem a name, because naming it is most of the fight.

## The test

The widow test is one question, and it has no partial credit:

**Could your least-technical heir recover your bitcoin — with you gone, with grief in the room, with a "helpful" relative hovering — using only what exists today?**

Read it again, slowly, because every clause is a trap.

*Least-technical heir.* Not your bitcoin buddy. The person who actually inherits — the one who calls you when the TV input changes.

*With you gone.* No phone call. No "honey, where's the—". Whatever you meant to explain someday, someday is over.

*Using only what exists today.* Not the system you're planning to set up. Not the document you've been meaning to write. Tonight's version. The real one.

Bitcoin has no forgot-password flow. There's no branch manager, no court order that can conjure a key, no customer support for the dead. That's the entire point of the asset — the same property that makes your coins unconfiscatable makes them unrecoverable. Sovereignty doesn't come with a help desk.

## Why the best bitcoiners fail it hardest

Here's the cruel joke at the center of this: **everything that makes you good at self-custody makes you bad at inheritance.**

Secrecy? Gold star for security — nobody knows what you hold or where. But nobody knowing means nobody can recover it either. The op-sec that defeats attackers defeats heirs by the exact same mechanism. The wrench can't find it; neither can the widow.

Complexity? Multisig, passphrases, steel plates distributed across three counties — beautiful. You've built a vault that requires *you* to operate. You are the single point of failure you spent all that money eliminating.

Distrust of third parties? Correct instinct, mostly. But "no custodian" also means no institution on Earth has standing to help your family. You fired the middlemen. All of them. Including the ones who used to handle exactly this.

The better you are at this game, the worse your score on the test. Sit with that one.

## The two ways to fail

Every inheritance plan fails in one of two directions, and the directions are at war with each other.

**Fail locked.** The keys die with you. By most estimates, millions of the 21 million coins are already unreachable forever — and some unknowable fraction of those are exactly this: somebody's careful security, outliving somebody. The coins don't go to your enemies, which is nice. They go to nobody, forever, which your family will not find philosophically comforting.

**Fail open.** The opposite sin: making recovery so easy it stops being yours. The seed phrase written in the will — which, in much of the world, becomes a *public document* when it's executed. The shoebox the cleaning service knows about. The "helpful" nephew who's good with computers and now knows everything. Distributing your key carelessly isn't an inheritance plan; it's just getting robbed on a delay.

And here's why this problem is genuinely hard, not just neglected: **every step toward recoverable is a step toward stealable.** That tension doesn't have a clever one-weird-trick solution. It has trade-offs, chosen deliberately, written down, and rehearsed.

## What passing actually smells like

I won't pretend to hand you a complete plan in a blog post — anyone who does is selling something. But having failed this test and started actually working the problem, I can tell you what passing smells like:

**The instructions live apart from the secrets.** Your heir needs a map, not a treasure chest with the map glued to it. A letter that says *what exists, where to start, who to trust, in what order* — containing nothing that helps a thief who finds it alone.

**It's been rehearsed.** This is the one almost everyone skips. Run the fire drill: your heir walks the recovery, alive-you in the next room, mouth shut, hands in pockets. The widow test is the only exam in life you can retake unlimited times *before* it counts — and almost nobody takes it even once. You will learn humbling things within ten minutes. ("What's a passphrase?" is where mine ended.)

**It survives a slow exit, not just a sudden one.** Morbid genius of the name aside, most people don't exit cleanly. Memory goes before breath does. A real plan works when *you're* the one who can't remember how your own vault operates — you are, in a sense, your own first widow.

**It rots, and you re-test it.** Wallets get migrated, services die, formats change — an inheritance plan decays exactly like [everything else I write about](/writing/your-zaps-are-probably-broken). The plan you made in 2021 points at apps that don't exist. Re-run the drill on a schedule, like the smoke detector it is.

## Run it this weekend

You don't need to solve inheritance this weekend. You need to *take the test* this weekend — honestly, with the actual heir, against the actual current state of your setup. Most of you will fail it. Good. Failing it now costs nothing. Failing it later costs everything, paid by the person you were protecting all along.

The exit we're all building toward doesn't mean much if it slams shut behind us.
