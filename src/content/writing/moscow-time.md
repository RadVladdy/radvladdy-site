The most expensive sentence in finance is seven words long, and you've probably heard it at a barbecue: *"I can't afford a whole bitcoin anyway."*

It sounds humble. It sounds sensible. It has cost the people who said it more than any market crash in history — because it's not a financial judgment, it's a *grammar mistake*. And the fix isn't a lecture about money printers or monetary history.

The fix is a clock.

## Nobody buys a whole anything

Quick: how much does gold cost?

If you said "about three thousand dollars," congratulations — you quoted the price of *an ounce*, a unit some guy picked centuries ago, and you didn't conclude that gold is "too expensive" for you. Nobody walks out of a jewelry store because they couldn't afford a standard 400-ounce vault bar. You buy a ring. A coin. A gram.

Pizza works the same way. So does electricity. So does literally everything — *except*, somehow, bitcoin, where otherwise rational adults look at the price of the largest possible retail unit and conclude the store is closed to them.

Here's the grammar lesson that fixes it: a bitcoin is not the unit. A bitcoin is the *vault bar*. The actual unit is the **sat** — there are one hundred million of them in every coin, the way there are a hundred cents in a dollar, and they are the denomination this entire economy actually runs on. Lightning invoices are priced in sats. Zaps are sats. The tip jar on this very page receives sats.

You cannot afford a vault bar. You have never needed one.

## So what's with the clock?

Flip the price upside down and something delightful happens.

Instead of asking *how many dollars for one bitcoin* — a number designed to make you feel late and poor — ask *how many sats for one dollar*. As I write this, the answer is about 1,600. And bitcoiners noticed years ago that this number, hovering in the four digits, reads like a time of day. 16:00. Quarter past four. A little joke formed and then calcified into slang, the way the best jokes do, and the inverted price got a name: **Moscow time** — the hour hand on the other side of the world from New York's dollar.

There's a clock like that on this site's homepage right now. It's not decoration. It's the single most useful reframe in all of bitcoin, because of what it does to the story:

Priced in dollars, bitcoin looks like a thing that got away from you — a train forever leaving the station.

Priced in sats, the *dollar* is the asset with the problem. Every four-year window since 2009, a dollar has bought fewer sats than the window before. The train isn't leaving the station. The station is sinking.

## Everyone you've ever envied thought they were late

This is the part I can back with receipts — my own.

In early 2020 I was [posting breathless arguments](/archive) for buying bitcoin *immediately*, and underneath every one of them was a quiet anxiety: the coin was nine thousand dollars, up from nothing, and I was clearly arriving after the party. The 2013 people felt late at a thousand. The 2011 people felt late at thirty dollars — *thirty* — because it had just run up from pennies. The 2017 crowd felt late at twenty grand, and then got four years of feeling stupid instead, which is the other half of the initiation everyone forgets to mention.

Every single cohort in bitcoin's history walked in feeling late, and every cohort was measuring with the same broken ruler: dollars per vault bar. It's a number that has gone up so relentlessly across the cycles that it permanently reads as *you missed it* — even though "it," the actual transformation, is maybe a tenth of the way through by any adoption measure that matters.

Now, honesty, because this site runs on it: the ride between any two points on that journey has included multiple drawdowns most people could not stomach — sixty, seventy, eighty percent, repeatedly, with everyone around you laughing. The clock doesn't spare you that. It just tells you what *unit* to do your suffering in.

## Not price advice. Unit advice.

Because here's what changing the denominator actually changes: your behavior.

"$30 of bitcoin" feels like a lottery ticket. "0.0005 BTC" feels like dust — a rounding error of a rounding error. But *fifty thousand sats* feels like what it is: **accumulation**. A number that grows when you add to it. A pile. Humans are wired to build piles, and completely unwired to celebrate the eighth decimal place.

That's why the verb in this culture is *stacking*. Nobody "allocates a fractional position." You stack sats — weekly, boringly, in amounts that don't frighten you, through prices that will definitely frighten you — and the unit itself keeps the score in a way your monkey brain can feel good about.

The people who figured this out aren't financial wizards. They just fixed their grammar.

## What time is it?

So the next time someone at the barbecue says the sentence — gently, because we were all that person — hand them the clock.

A dollar buys about 1,600 sats today. It bought more last cycle. It will buy fewer next cycle, not because of magic, but because one side of that ratio gets printed by the trillion and the other side has a fixed supply with a halving schedule old enough to drive.

You're not too late. You're not even early. You're just looking at the wrong clock.

There's a live one on my homepage. It doesn't care when you arrived. ⚡
